The region

Tuscany, as Chiara teaches it

Chiara was born in Fiesole, above Florence, and grew up walking down the hill to school past cypress rows that appear in half the paintings you have already seen. She read literature at Firenze, taught in Barcelona for two years, then came home. Her Italian is standard Tuscan, the source Italian, the one from which the language on television and in newspapers is drawn. Her English is warm and careful and does not compete with the Italian for space in the class.

Every teacher on our faculty teaches from one Italian region and from one only. That is not a marketing point. It is what allows a class to have a place. When Chiara opens a lesson at seven in the morning her time, the light behind her is Firenze light. The bread on her desk was bought at a Firenze bakery. The vocabulary that emerges in a conversation about weekends is Tuscany vocabulary. Students on the other side of the world tell us the same thing, class after class. They are not on a language platform. They are in Firenze, for eighty five minutes.

Regional Italian is not dialect. Chiara teaches the standard national language, the Italian of newspapers and universities and the state broadcaster. What she brings is location. A verb form is illustrated with a scene from her city. A grammar point is drawn out of a conversation that could have taken place on her street. The language stays standard. The teaching room is specific.

Teaching manifesto
I teach adults. Adults come with a life already written in another language, and my job is not to erase it. My job is to give them a second interior voice, one that speaks Italian back to them when they are washing dishes or driving home. That is the sign the language has taken root. So I move slowly on the first weeks. I resist the temptation to fill silence. I ask questions I actually want the answer to. When a student in Chicago tells me about her father in ninety careful Italian words, that is a class I remember for a year. Grammar is a bookshelf. Speech is a house you live in. I build the house first, and we add the shelves as we go. My students in Florence, my students in San Francisco and my students in Buenos Aires all get the same me. That consistency is the offer.
Chiara Bellini · Firenze

A typical lesson with Chiara

Eighty five minutes, structured for adults

01

Learning goals

  • One grammar point named at the start of the lesson
  • Ten to fifteen new items of Tuscany-anchored vocabulary
  • One conversational function students will leave able to perform
  • One short cultural note tied to the day's material
  • A named piece of work to bring back next week
02

The eighty five minute flow

  • 0 to 10 · warm up in Italian, weekend and week ahead
  • 10 to 25 · new material, introduced through a real scene
  • 25 to 45 · guided practice, in pairs on breakout
  • 45 to 55 · a short break, Chiara stays online
  • 55 to 75 · free conversation on the day's theme
  • 75 to 85 · review, homework, questions
03

Between lesson work

  • One short reading from Chiara's Italian sources
  • One audio, usually a real recording under three minutes
  • One written piece, no longer than 120 words
  • A quiet fifteen minutes with Biagio, if you want it
  • Total realistic weekly load: ninety to a hundred minutes
