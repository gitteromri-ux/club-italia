Sei capitoli. Un paese.

Six chapters in the
Italian art of living.

Italy is not a country. It is twenty regions, three thousand years, and a shared conviction that beauty, food, cinema, and time itself should be arranged with care. Our syllabus follows the six chapters that hold it all together.

01

The Thesis

A language you can only enter through a door.

The other Latin languages travel light. Italian travels with luggage. It arrives with its cathedrals, its feast days, its endless small towns where every hill has its own church and its own dessert. To learn Italian in a vocabulary list is to learn a shadow of it. To learn it properly, you enter one of six rooms and speak from inside.

These are the six rooms. Every Club Italia course crosses each of them at least once. By the end of your season, you have not travelled to Italy in the tourist sense. You have moved into a small apartment there, hung your coat, and been introduced.

i

Chapter One

Arte, or the country that invented looking up.

Every Italian city holds the record for something. Florence taught the West how to paint a face. Rome taught it how to build a dome. Venice taught it how a colour behaves when it sits next to another. And in every case, the lesson was public. Frescoes went on the walls of the town hall, not the private chapel. The Duomo was for the plumber and the countess in the same afternoon.

This is why Italian art is a civic language, and why speaking Italian well means learning to speak about beauty without embarrassment. In Club Italia we visit the Uffizi with a curator, but also a graffiti wall in Bologna with the artist who signed it. Renaissance and now, the same syllabus.

ii

Chapter Two

Cucina, a grammar you can eat.

There is no such thing as Italian food. There is Bolognese food, and Sicilian food, and Ligurian food, and a hundred more, and they do not always agree with each other about basic questions such as what belongs in a pasta sauce. This is not chaos. It is a federation. A regional grammar in which every province holds a veto.

In our cucina chapter, we do not teach you to cook. We teach you to order, to argue about ingredients with the shopkeeper, and to sit down at an Italian table without giving offence. That is a language skill in Italy, and it is a serious one.

Napoli, la pizzaThe word that changed the world's dinner.
Bologna, il ragùWhy the recipe is filed at the town hall.

iii

Chapter Three

Viaggio, twenty countries in one passport.

Italy has been unified for 165 years. That is roughly the age of one great-grandmother. Before that, it was a peninsula of principalities, republics, and kingdoms, each of which is still visible in the accent of anyone speaking on a train. A conversation in Trento does not sound like one in Palermo, and neither of them sounds much like Milan.

Our travel chapter is the syllabus of these differences. Each Club Italia course visits three or four regions in depth. You do not just learn where they are. You learn how the language changes when you cross into them, which vowels lengthen, which consonants soften, which words vanish and which appear.

Puglia, il taccoThe white towns and the almond harvest.
Trentino, il nordWhere Italian meets its German neighbour.

iv

Chapter Four

Cinema, the picture teachers of modern Italian.

If you want to learn how modern Italians actually speak, you cannot do better than an evening with a good film. It is faster than a textbook and more truthful than a phrasebook. Fellini, Rossellini, Sorrentino, Rohrwacher, all of them wrote dialogue that adults use, in rooms adults inhabit. You can hear the difference between a Roman and a Neapolitan within a minute.

Every Club Italia course includes at least three film units. We do not watch clips. We watch full scenes, slow them down, learn the phrases, and then rehearse them in class. By Avanzato, you can follow La Grande Bellezza without subtitles.

Roma, cinecittàThe studios that made mid-century Italian.
Napoli, SorrentinoModern melancholy, in the accent of the city.
Reading a Sorrentino scene aloud, Avanzato · Week Nine.
v

Chapter Five

Opera, the libretto the world already knows.

Every English speaker already knows more Italian than they think. Piano, forte, allegro, crescendo, aria, tempo. These are not loans. They are still Italian, still doing their job. Opera gave us the vocabulary of music because for two centuries opera was the way Europe understood what music could do.

Our opera chapter takes you inside three librettos: Verdi's Traviata, Puccini's Tosca, Rossini's Barbiere. We translate the arias line by line. You leave able to follow the plot of an opera in real time, in Italian, without the surtitles.

Milano, La ScalaThe theatre that set the standard.
Verona, l'arenaOpera in the open, since 1913.

vi

Chapter Six

Tradizioni, the calendar that holds Italian time.

Italian time is different from northern European time. It is arranged around feast days, patron saints, harvests, and the great national pause of Ferragosto in mid-August, when the country closes and moves to the sea. To speak Italian well, you have to know when it is Pasquetta and why nobody is answering your emails.

The tradition chapter is the practical calendar of Italian life. Christmas Eve in Naples. The Palio in Siena. La Festa della Repubblica. August in a mountain village. You leave the season knowing when Italy is working, when it is celebrating, and when, quite properly, it is doing neither.

Napoli, il NataleThe presepe, the pasticceria, the vigil.
Siena, il PalioThe horse race that is a city argument.
